# Glossary

**3 A's** — Admonishments, advice, and apologies. The three add-ons that most often ruin a BIFF. (Ch 3)

**Admonishment** — A personal criticism from a one-up position ("you should know better"). Heard as an attack on the whole person. (Ch 3)

**Advice** — Unsolicited suggestions. Treated as disrespect unless the person asked. (Ch 3)

**All-or-nothing thinking** — One person all good, another all bad; compromise feels dangerous. An HCP pattern. (Ch 1, Ch 11)

**Amygdala** — Eddy's shorthand for the fast alarm that grabs attention and crowds out careful thought. He is not writing as a neuroscientist. (Ch 3)

**Apology (substantive)** — Taking responsibility for causing the problem. HCPs often store it as proof it was all your fault. Social apologies are different. (Ch 3)

**BIFF** — Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm. A short written or spoken response meant to inform and disengage without triggering defensiveness. (Ch 2)

**Blamespeak** — Hostile, personal, out-of-proportion language that assigns all fault to a target and often shares that story with others. (Ch 1)

**Containment** — Keeping contact narrow and calm (often by email) so it does not move to the workplace door, the courts, or a public scene. (Ch 6)

**Counter-Blamespeak** — Attacking back in kind. Often true, and still escalates, and makes you look like the high-conflict person to outsiders. (Ch 1)

**Easy way or hard way** — Two choices with consequences, so the person decides, instead of a single threat. (Ch 8)

**Emotions are contagious** — Intense feeling spreads faster than analysis. Alarming opinions get adopted before they are checked. (Ch 7)

**Fast defensive reacting** — Eddy's "right-brain" mode: all-or-nothing, crisis, bad people, no compromise. (Ch 11)

**Firm** — The reply ends the topic, or sets two choices and a date, without a new argument. (Ch 2)

**Friendly** — A respectful tone that lowers alarm. Thanks, acknowledgment, or a neutral closing. Not sarcasm. (Ch 2)

**High-conflict person (HCP)** — Eddy's descriptive term for someone who repeatedly increases conflict through blaming, extreme emotion, and extreme behavior, with little self-awareness or self-change. Not a diagnosis. (Ch 1)

**Indirect confrontation** — Ask for future behavior and give a policy reason, instead of attacking past character. (Ch 6)

**Informative** — Neutral facts that move the exchange onto a decidable subject. (Ch 2)

**Logical problem-solving** — Eddy's "left-brain" mode: flexible, contextual, open to compromise, emotions managed. (Ch 11)

**Make it small** — Do not add readers the attacker did not actually include. (Ch 7)

**Negative advocates** — People recruited to push an HCP's blame. Usually emotionally hooked and uninformed. (Ch 5)

**Negative feedback** — Criticism that hits the whole person, uses a harsh tone, or stays stuck in the past. Tends to fail with HCPs. (Ch 7)

**Personality disorder** — A clinical diagnosis. Only a qualified professional makes it. Overlaps with HCP behavior sometimes, and often does not. Do not announce it. (Ch 1)

**Phasing out** — Ending contact slowly by limiting specific requests, so an abrupt cutoff does not increase pursuit. (Ch 2, Ch 4)

**Private working theory** — Your unstated hypothesis that someone may be an HCP, used only to choose your own response. (Ch 1)

**Projection** — An HCP attributing their own behavior to you. Do not point it out in the reply. (Ch 6)

**Target of blame** — The person an HCP preoccupies on. It may be you, especially if you are close to them or in authority. (Ch 1)

**The issue's not the issue** — The stated complaint is often the personality pattern, not the tree, the invoice, or the policy. Still answer the practical point. (Ch 5, Ch 9)

**Two choices** — A limit that preserves a decision: two options, stated calmly, with a short deadline. (Ch 8)
