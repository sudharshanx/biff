# Chapter 3: Avoid Admonishments, Advice and Apologies

## Core Idea
The draft fails in what you add after the facts. Admonishments, advice, and apologies each tell a defensive person that you sit above them or that the fault is entirely yours.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **3 A's**: Admonishments, Advice, Apologies.
  - When to use: on every draft, after the BIFF check.
  - How: delete judgment ("you should know better," "look in the mirror"). Delete suggestions they did not request. Delete "I'm sorry I caused this." Keep "I'm sorry I'm late" and "I'm sorry you are dealing with this."
- **Talk to the alarm, then the problem**: Eddy maps fast defense to a "right-brain" reaction and careful problem-solving to a "left-brain" one. He is explicit that this is practical, not a neuroscience paper.
  - When to use: you are emotionally hooked and about to type the counter-attack.
  - How: pause. Use a short line: "This is not about me." A calm, informative, friendly note gives the other person less alarm to organize around. You will still get hooked sometimes. Catch it sooner.
- **Retrain the reply**: practice removes Blamespeak the way practice changes any skill.
  - When to use: the urge to explain, advise, or apologize feels helpful.
  - How: write the satisfying draft, then cut it to a BIFF before anyone sees it. A second reader spots the 3 A's you have gone blind to.

## Key Concepts
- **Admonishment**: parent-voice or judge-voice. The offense is the assumption that you may grade them.
- **Advice**: "just trying to help" still ranks you above them if they did not ask.
- **Apology stored as proof**: a worn note from years ago gets read back as your confession.
- **Social nicety vs substance**: late-for-coffee is fine. "I was wrong to criticize you and I will be a better person" will be reused.
- **Emotionally hooked**: the urge is normal. Sending it is optional.

## Mental Models
- Use "what I leave out matters more than what I say" as the editing rule.
- Think of an apology to an HCP as ammunition, not as repair.
- Think of social media as a room where bystanders join in minutes. A friend can post a BIFF and shrink the pile-on, or can add another insult and grow it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Advising an adult child about their partner's job search**: intrusive, even when the research is accurate.
- **Apologizing for "bothering" someone who asked to be left alone**: they will quote you.
- **Repeating an offer they refused**: it lands as "I still think you cannot cope."

## Worked Example
An adult son writes that he is a grownup, his partner is the issue from the parent's side, and they do not want money or a visit.

First draft: he is short-sighted; the parent has found jobs for the partner; please reconsider the visit; "I don't want to bother you." That draft is brief-ish and sounds kind. It is an admonishment, unsolicited advice, and an apology. It is not firm. The quoted warmth is slightly sarcastic.

Better: thanks for answering. You respect his privacy and want him to live his own life. Since you lost your partner, family contact matters. A call or an email now and then keeps you from worrying and feeling you should check on him. You are glad they are happy. Have a nice weekend. The firm part is your action (you may feel like visiting if you hear nothing), not an order. Drop the money offer after he refused it. In the version that worked, the financial sentence came out, and he called.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the 3 A's after you think the draft is already short and kind.
2. Unsolicited help is still a rank move.
3. Substantive apologies get archived.
4. A reviewer earns their place on this chapter more than on any other.
