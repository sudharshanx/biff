# Chapter 2: Writing a BIFF Response

## Core Idea
A BIFF is a short, neutral, respectful close. It informs and disengages. It is usually the opposite of what you feel like sending.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **BIFF**: Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm.
  - When to use: you have decided a response is necessary.
  - How: one paragraph, 2–5 sentences. A sentence or two of straight information, no opinions about each other. A friendly open or close. A firm ending, or two choices plus a reply date. "If I don't have this by Friday, I will file it without your comment. I hope that won't be necessary."
- **Do you need to respond?**: silence is often best.
  - When to use: only the two of you; no real issue; pure opinion about you; you will not change their mind; you already answered.
  - How: do nothing. Do not send a short insult instead.
- **Reply fast, same people, same format**:
  - When to use: others heard it, or an HCP will treat silence as agreement.
  - How: email for email, Reply All for a group, a public note for a public attack. If their channel will distort you, use a channel you control that reaches the same community. Have a reviewer cut the draft, especially in public.
- **Three goals**: manage, reduce, or end.
  - When to use: before you choose tone and how much future contact you offer.
  - How: negative feedback intensifies contact. Abrupt endings often increase pursuit. Phase out. Emphasize future behavior over the past.

## Key Concepts
- **Brief**: their length does not set yours. More words, more hooks.
- **Informative**: objective subject, neutral terms. If the only issue is personality, still offer a related fact and skip the character debate.
- **Friendly**: "Thank you for telling me your opinion." Avoid sarcasm when their note was vicious. "Okay, [name]" is enough.
- **Firm**: this is all you will say. A second ask reopens the attack.
- **Reviewer cuts it in half**: normal. Use it.

## Mental Models
- Use BIFF when you want relief and a closed thread, not a twenty-minute high.
- Think of silence as consent inside their defensive logic, and as a fact to anyone they tell.
- Think of the goal question as: will this sentence pull them further into my life, or let them step back without defensiveness?

## Anti-patterns
- **Answering every allegation**: you will not change their mind. You will renew the fight.
- **Friendly words with a sarcastic quote**: "glad you found 'the woman you love'" is not friendly.
- **Going over a boss's head by calling them a liar**: correct the work facts. Do not narrate the betrayal.

## Worked Example
A parent asks to take the children to a short, alcohol-free office party during the other parent's time. The reply is two pages of insults and a refusal.

BIFF: thank them for answering. State the hours, the headcount, and that other children will be there. Say you will respect the refusal and withdraw the request. Stop. Do not deny the affair, the finances, or the memory. If friends were copied, one group note: private matter, almost all of it inaccurate, contact you with questions.

A separate workplace shape: a boss emails a project manager that you are dragging your feet, and copies you. Your note to the project manager says the analysis is nearly done, you followed the schedule, and the result is due Friday. No sentence says the boss lied. You told the boss you were reassuring the client about the deadline. Then you changed the subject.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decide the goal, then decide whether to write at all.
2. One paragraph. Facts. Respect. A closed door.
3. Match the audience that already heard the attack. Do not invent a larger one.
4. Put the written reply where the file will outlast the mood.
