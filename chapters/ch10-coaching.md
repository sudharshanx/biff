# Chapter 10: Coaching for BIFF Responses

## Core Idea
Coach the writer to judge their own draft. Your clever rewrite makes you look useful and leaves them unable to do the next one.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **10 questions**, in this order:
  1. Is it Brief?
  2. Is it Informative?
  3. Is it Friendly?
  4. Is it Firm?
  5. Does it contain any Advice?
  6. Does it contain any Admonishments?
  7. Does it contain any Apologies?
  8. How do you think the other person will respond?
  9. Is there anything you would take out, add, or change?
  10. Would you like to hear my thoughts?
  - When to use: any time you review a BIFF, including for a friend. You are not their lawyer and not their therapist in this role.
  - How: they read it aloud. You do not suggest edits during questions 1–9. "Okay" and "looks that way to me too" are enough. If they ask for your view early, say you will come back after the ten. Question 10 happens only if they say yes.
- **The BIFF belongs to the writer**: like cooking, theirs will not match yours. There is no single correct version. It depends on who writes, who receives, and the situation.
  - When to use: you are sure your sentence is better.
  - How: offer two or three phrasings, then "what do you think?" If they ask "how should I say it?", ask them to try first.
- **Vulnerable writer**: they are being attacked elsewhere. A hint of personal criticism from you lands hard.
  - When to use: your first impulse is to mark up the prose.
  - How: be encouraging on the early answers. Save the one important cut for the end. Do not flood them with twelve ideas. The best session is the one where they feel smart.

## Key Concepts
- **Explain the 3 A's only if they have forgotten**: advice they were not asked for; parent-to-child admonishment; substantive apology that becomes ammunition. Social apologies are allowed.
- **Read it both ways**: with the risky phrase and without. Let them hear the difference.
- **It's up to you**: the line you repeat so you do not take over their relationship.
- **Don't work harder than the client**: you are responsible for the method, not for their outcome.

## Mental Models
- Use question 10's placement as the discipline. If it were first, you would skip their learning.
- Think of a trigger phrase ("using you," "no need to be impatient") as the sentence the other person will answer, no matter how good the rest is.
- Think of a friendly last line as a pat on the back that can keep a touchy person useful.

## Anti-patterns
- **Correcting the draft the moment you see it**: they never practice the check.
- **One imposed sentence**: they send your email, then cannot write the next.
- **Coaching to look brilliant**: Eddy flags this directly. The coach is not the point.

## Worked Example
A student draft says they agree to stop doing math homework together, denies they were "using" the friend, and wants to stay friends.

After the ten questions, you say the "using you" phrase may become the next attack ("I figured the assignment out"). Ask whether that clause, or the whole sentence, can go. They object that they cannot let the accusation stand. You say you doubt this BIFF will prove it, and they can raise it later in a friendly conversation if they need to. They read the note without that sentence and agree it sounds better. You stop. You do not add a moral.

A manager's draft about a burned-out streetlight is already brief and factual, plus "there's no need to be impatient." He hears it both ways and cuts that phrase himself. You then offer one optional ending: everyone wants the block well lit, thanks again for reporting it. He keeps it because it rewards a useful act.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ten questions, their analysis first, your ideas last and only if invited.
2. Suggestions are tentative and come in pairs.
3. One risky phrase can be the whole problem.
4. Repeat that it is their BIFF and their situation.
