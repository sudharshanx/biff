# Chapter 5: Neighbors

## Core Idea
A neighbor dispute escalates because the person lives next door and can recruit the block. Answer the escalation with a joint next step, then answer the recruited neighbors before the story sets.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two choices toward a fix**: instead of a second, angrier no.
  - When to use: you already refused, and they escalate with lawyers, property values, or "everyone agrees."
  - How: say you want a workable neighbor relationship. Offer a community mediator. Give the number. They can call, or you can. Ask which. No review of their character, no counter-threat.
- **Negative advocates**: people an HCP recruits to carry a negative solution, emotion, or behavior.
  - When to use: your reply gets forwarded with a plea to pressure you.
  - How: respond fast, same channel. You see they were contacted. Here is the suggestion already made. Questions come to you. You like the quiet block and expect to solve this. Stop. Do not attack the forwarder.
- **Hooked but uninformed**: the working picture of a negative advocate.
  - When to use: you are angry at the neighbors for "taking a side."
  - How: inform them. Consultation for you is not the same as recruiting a side. Sometimes you leave advocates alone because you cannot reach them safely.

## Key Concepts
- **Email vs in person**: ordinary neighbor friction is often better spoken. A high-conflict risk is a reason to use a mediator instead of a doorway argument.
- **Verbal BIFF**: a board chair can do the same four moves out loud, in a few sentences, then call the next speaker.
- **Where results go**: posting an outcome on a website can avoid giving a weak complaint a second meeting.

## Mental Models
- Use "the issue's not the issue" when the trees, the fence, or the litter is the stage and the pattern is the show. Still answer the practical point.
- Think of a quick factual note as what keeps a neighbor from absorbing only the alarmed version.
- Think of mediation as a way to be firm without demanding that they obey you.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counter-threat plus "find something better to do"**: brief, and still an admonishment, advice, and a dare.
- **Naming the neighbor in public** while they named you: you can correct the record without copying their tactic.
- **Waiting days** so the emotion can become the block's opinion.

## Worked Example
Up-the-hill neighbor demands treetops come off, says a lawyer agrees, and says two neighbors agree. Your first impulse lists ten years of peace, doubts the other neighbors exist, and threatens your own lawyer.

BIFF: you are sorry this is frustrating (not sorry for the trees). You want both households calm. You suggest the community mediation service, give the number, and offer either of you placing the call. Regards.

When that note is forwarded: a short note to those two neighbors. You see the disagreement. Your earlier email already proposed a way to resolve it. They can talk to you anytime. You expect the block to stay peaceful. After that, most people work hard not to join.

At a homeowners meeting, a resident accuses the manager of litter and fence favoritism and gives the board a week. The chair: thanks, this had not been raised, it will be looked into, the result will be posted, feedback is welcome, next speaker. No public trial that night.

## Key Takeaways
1. A second refusal should narrow to a process, not a louder character review.
2. Negative advocates need information immediately, in the channel they received the story.
3. Informative is the useful move against people who are upset and under-informed.
4. A verbal BIFF can be four sentences and then the next agenda item.
