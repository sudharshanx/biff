# Chapter 11: You Decide

## Core Idea
Your reply helps decide which mode they stay in. Short, neutral, warm, and finished points them at problem-solving. A long attack keeps them in defense, where they already get stuck.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Logical problem-solving vs fast defensive reacting**: Eddy's memory picture, loosely tied to left and right hemispheres. He repeats that this is a practical summary, not a lab claim.
  - When to use: you need to remember why the four BIFF rules exist.
  - How: problem-solving is flexible, treats the situation as complex behavior, keeps context, expects compromise, and can hold fear without obeying it. Fast defense is all-or-nothing, treats it as a crisis and as bad people, isolates the issue, treats compromise as dangerous, and only calms down by striking. HCPs slip into the second and return slowly. Anyone slips into it if the moment feels threatening enough.
- **Each BIFF word has a job in that picture**:
  - Brief: less negative material to trip the alarm.
  - Informative: neutral facts give the analytical mode something to hold.
  - Friendly: threat locks all-or-nothing thinking. Warmth can move them back.
  - Firm: once they are calm and respected, a clear ending lets them stop defending, which lets them stop attacking.
- **Turn down Blamespeak in your own diet**: radio, television, and feeds train your mood.
  - When to use: you notice you are more anxious and faster to snap.
  - How: feelings are contagious, and Blamespeak is built to trip the alarm. Prefer people and sources that hand you positive, logical information. You cannot avoid all of it. You can stop marinating in it.
- **Coach with the same method**: share the principles without a lecture. Ten questions. "It's up to you."

## Key Concepts
- **You may decide what they think about next**: the topic of your reply becomes their next picture.
- **Don't delay a needed reply**: speed matters when others are forming a conclusion. Still choose silence when silence is the better BIFF.
- **Power displays backfire**: looking strong feels good to you and reads as a threat to someone already defensive.
- **Non-threatening and reasonable**: the stance that makes the method work.

## Mental Models
- Use the two-column picture when you are about to write something "strong." Ask which column it feeds.
- Think of a BIFF as doing the gear-shift they cannot do while flooded.
- Think of your media diet as part of the skill. A flooded writer writes flooded email.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trying to appear powerful**: it triggers the defense you wanted to end.
- **A long coaching speech**: when you review someone else's draft, your feedback should itself be a BIFF.
- **Treating the brain chapter as permission to diagnose**: it is a memory aid for your wording.

## Worked Example
Two columns, used as a pre-send check.

You are about to write: "If you don't stop threatening us, we will have you arrested if you set foot here, and we have done nothing wrong."

That draft feeds fast defense. It is about bad people, it isolates the threat, and it gives them the picture of showing up.

Shift: "Glad you are getting interviews. The job-duty list is attached. Best wishes." The arrest plan, if you need one, happens with security, off the page. The email is brief, factual, warm, and finished. Their next thought has somewhere calm to go.

Eddy closes the book by saying he tried to keep it brief, informative, friendly, and firm. Hold your own drafts to that test.

## Key Takeaways
1. The four words are tools for shifting someone out of alarm, including you.
2. What you mention becomes what they picture next.
3. Cut Blamespeak out of your inputs when you can.
4. Coaching stays light: ten questions, then stop.
