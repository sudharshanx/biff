# Chapter 4: BIFFs for Friends and Family (and Exes)

## Core Idea
Close relationships are where you usually cannot just leave. Use BIFF to manage a sibling or co-parent, and to narrow a friendship until it fades, without a dramatic ending.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Narrow help instead of rescue**: with a dependent relative who wants you to absorb the whole crisis.
  - When to use: you have already said no to moving in, paying, or fixing the boss.
  - How: do not re-defend the no. Do not correct the insult you never said. Offer one next step you will actually do (a list of rentals, a few calls). Next time, point back at that step or say you have nothing new.
- **Phase out by specific limits**: friendships with HCPs can feel like family and end as nastily as a divorce.
  - When to use: you want less contact and a sudden breakup may spike pursuit or a crisis.
  - How: say no to this visit, this week, this plan. Skip the history of how self-centered they are. Reducing often beats a single "we are done" speech. A counselor can hold a structured ending if you need one.
- **Stay focused**: they will not answer the question. They will open a new attack.
  - When to use: a scheduling request comes back as a threat about homework, money, or your character.
  - How: one clause joining their real concern, then return to your request. State what you will do. Leave them a way to say no. If they keep writing: "I've said all I'm going to say on this subject." Then stop.

## Key Concepts
- **Distraction**: move a stuck person onto a concrete next task instead of the insult.
- **Should vs could**: "you should look at listings" is advice-shaped. "You could" is lighter. Neither requires you to take them in.
- **Record**: a short, reasonable parenting BIFF is also what you would want a later reader to see.
- **Suicide statements**: some HCPs say this often, and the risk is still real. People who die by suicide often told someone first.

## Mental Models
- Use email when you need time to calm down, and only if you have not already promised silence.
- Think of "either you're with me or against me" as a hook, not a decision you must accept.
- Think of a faded friendship as a series of nos, not a verdict on their childhood.

## Anti-patterns
- **The long honesty letter**: it intensifies the bond you are trying to thin.
- **Arguing a misquote** ("you said I was a speck"): you will not win it, and you re-trigger victim stance.
- **Letting a suicide line decide the unrelated request**: take the safety path, and still hold the limit.

## Worked Example
A friend announces a week-long stay, catalogs betrayals, and adds that some mornings they feel like ending it. You are burned out and dating someone new.

BIFF shape: thanks for the dates. The next stretch is a bad time because you are with a new partner, and you are asking them not to come. Ask them to confirm they got that. Separately, name the suicide sentence, point them at the EAP, a counselor, or the hotline you already gave, and say that if you do not hear within a set window that they are talking to someone, you will call their mother or the police. That second part follows a plan you already made with a counselor. The first time you heard this kind of talk, calling the police was the move. Do not use the visit as the price of their safety, and do not debate the visit inside the safety paragraph.

If you do not know which path fits, contact a hotline or a counselor before you send anything.

## Key Takeaways
1. Manage the relationships you cannot leave by narrowing the topic.
2. End friendships by winding down specific requests, not by a closing argument.
3. Expect the subject to change. Return to yours once, in writing.
4. Suicide talk gets a safety response every time, including when you have heard it before.
