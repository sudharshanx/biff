# Chapter 9: Politicians

## Core Idea
Blamespeak wins attention in the short run and wears out the speaker in the long run. A public BIFF answers a specific false charge with shared concern, facts, and a turn toward the future, without naming the attacker.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Public BIFF**: for a specific accusation already in front of the audience (fraud, a named crime, a decision you did not make).
  - When to use: credible misinformation is spreading and silence will be treated as agreement.
  - How: appreciate the concern. Share it. State who reviewed the decisions and that they did not find fraud, including decisions you were not part of. Say extreme words need evidence. Credit that the other person wants the best for the community. Say you will focus on the future. Then stop answering that charge.
- **Ignore vague campaign lines**: "one of the most corrupt" with no fact, no investigation, and no live issue.
  - When to use: the line is still stump speech.
  - How: let it pass. If a specific allegation appears, switch to the public BIFF. Reporters asking questions are the better first response at national scale.
- **Don't make it personal**: in public, avoid their name and avoid grading their character.
  - When to use: you want the high road and you also want the record corrected.
  - How: discuss the concern and your principle. Negative advocates are hooked and uninformed. Educate them. Anger at them confirms the enemy picture.

## Key Concepts
- **Hero against villains**: a common HCP political opening. All-or-nothing, extreme emotion, a promise that only they can fix it. Government is messier than that.
- **Those who live by Blamespeak often crash by it**: predictable over-attacking spends credibility.
- **Counter-Blamespeak in public**: "look in the mirror" on the steps makes the feud the story.
- **The issue's not the issue**: a commentator may eventually say the personality is the conflict. You still answer the factual charge once.

## Mental Models
- Use certainty-plus-alarm as the thing you are racing. People follow a confident emotional leader even when the claim is wrong, especially when they are already anxious.
- Think of repeated facial fear and anger on screens as an alarm that crowds out later corrections. Days later is often too late.
- Think of one calm statement as enough. Further replies look like the war.

## Anti-patterns
- **Answering every epithet**: you feed the cycle and look like a bully.
- **Naming them** so your clip becomes the next ad.
- **Waiting for the public to logically dismiss a fraud claim**: anxious audiences absorb the feeling first.

## Worked Example
A city attorney publicly says the mayor made false and misleading statements about pensions, during a season of pension investigations. The mayor was not part of those earlier decisions. The attorney has a reputation for a high volume of losing fights. The mayor's angry "look in the mirror" made it a "civil war."

BIFF at the next public chance: you share the concern about the city's finances. Auditors looked at decisions you were not involved in and did not find fraud or crime. Extreme words need evidence. You believe the city attorney wants the best for the city. You will focus on the future. Then do not re-engage. In Eddy's telling, the attorney's wider pattern of accusations later cost him the election, and the mayor's restraint held up better than the insult would have.

A national politician's broad "most corrupt president" line, with no personal fact and a later walk-back to "business as usual," can be left alone. If an investigation is actually open, use the same paragraph as the mayor, aimed at the nation's finances, still without a name-calling match.

## Key Takeaways
1. Specific public accusations get one factual BIFF. Vague insults can be ignored.
2. Do not say their name. Do not review their personality in public.
3. Inform alarmed listeners. Do not scold them for being alarmed.
4. Restraint is the long campaign. Blamespeak is the short one.
