# Chapter 6: In the Workplace

## Core Idea
At work you often cannot leave the thread. Write so the next behavior is clear, the policy is the reason, and the person's character is not the topic.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Indirect confrontation**: desired future behavior, justified by a policy, schedule, or external pressure.
  - When to use: direct criticism makes them explode or walk out, and you still need the behavior.
  - How: say what "on time," "this parking rule," and "this completion date" mean. Skip "you are a jerk." If discipline is required, do it through the formal path. Early and small beats a late character memo.
- **Resist pointing out projection**: they put their own trait on you ("Princess") and do not see it.
  - When to use: the nickname is tempting to unpack.
  - How: ignore it. Defending the label keeps the conversation alive.
- **Contain after exit**: a terminated employee may keep emailing. That channel is often safer than a visit or a rumor campaign.
  - When to use: HR is still getting hostile mail with a practical ask mixed in.
  - How: encourage the job search in one line. Attach the document. Stop. No speech about threats. Separately, tell security if they said they might come by, and remove the reason to come by.

## Key Concepts
- **Carrot for a narcissistic-style boss**: policies apply to the unit, including them, and you will help the unit look good. Eddy treats this as a common workplace pattern, not a diagnosis you should announce.
- **Do not challenge a status-sensitive boss head-on**: they often punish the challenge for a long time.
- **Pink elephant**: "don't you dare come back" makes them picture coming back. Leave the scene out of the BIFF.
- **Friendly length**: with someone you must keep working with, a slightly warmer paragraph can sound less hostile than a curt one. Still one paragraph.

## Mental Models
- Use policy as the stick and reputation as the carrot when the boss invents rules.
- Think of the BIFF to the project manager as protecting the file: a later reader should see your facts beside the accusation.
- Think of post-termination email as a container, not a relationship you must win.

## Anti-patterns
- **The double-standard inventory**: your sick days versus theirs, the favorite employee, a threat to report them. Justified, and it escalates.
- **"It will not benefit you to threaten us"**: an admonishment that invites the next email.
- **Promising arrest inside the note**: handle the safety step offline. The note stays boring.

## Worked Example
A manager emails "Princess" about one sick day, demands a doctor's note, and hints at replacement. You were out with a short stomach flu. Your child was too.

BIFF: thanks for raising the absence. You were ill, it lasted a day, you came back because the presentation matters, you were not told a note was required the last time you missed a day, please tell the unit if that is the rule, and you are ready to help the presentation make the unit look good. No comparison of sick days. No harassment warning in this email.

A terminated employee demands a job-duty list, blames you for a ruined career, and hints at dropping by. BIFF: glad the interviews are happening, you want a better fit for them, the list is attached, best wishes. Security hears the "drop by" separately.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask for the next behavior and cite the rule.
2. Do not explain their projection.
3. After someone leaves, contain the mail. Do not replay the threats inside it.
4. Teach the method widely so coworkers stop escalating each other.
