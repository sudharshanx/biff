# Chapter 7: Business and Professions

## Core Idea
You will not screen out high-conflict clients, vendors, or partners at the start. Contain the emotion, keep the service, and answer public alarms before they become the market's opinion.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Make it small**: do not copy people the sender only claimed to have on their side.
  - When to use: "my lawyer and my spouse agree" appears in a letter that copied nobody.
  - How: reply only to the sender. Adding the lawyer makes them ask questions, which makes the sender more defensive. HCPs often invent unanimous allies. If a copy was real, answer those recipients. If you are unsure, stay small.
- **Avoid negative feedback's three ingredients**: whole-person attack, harsh tone, past-only focus.
  - When to use: a partner yells, bad-mouths you, and ignores verbal warnings.
  - How: open with genuine respect for a real skill. Limit the list to the few behaviors that matter. State the future behavior. State a real consequence (counseling sessions, or a wind-down of the partnership) and that you hope not to use it. A list of "do nots" is not a BIFF even if the facts are true.
- **Fast public BIFF**: alarming opinions spread faster than analysis.
  - When to use: a vivid public incident is being believed as the whole company.
  - How: short, customer-facing, factual, about what you are doing now. Do not spend the message destroying the employees. Eddy cites a pizza company whose president answered a viral video in under two minutes: isolated incident, customer trust, the company still stands. Sales recovered.

## Key Concepts
- **Unrealistic expectations**: the business risk is complaints, boards, rumors, and lawsuits, not only a rude email.
- **Goal**: contain and keep serving. Most HCPs can still be customers or sources of work if the emotion is contained.
- **Different styles**: a professional can say people want different styles, wish them well, and stop, without a refund debate in the first letter.
- **They eventually show the pattern**: you often do not need to warn the next professional. The client will attack that person too.

## Mental Models
- Use "don't make them think about the refund" when mentioning the demand would revive it. Be ready with a second, firmer BIFF if they write again.
- Think of intense group emotion as a wireless network. People adopt "everyone believes it" before they check.
- Think of six unresolved grievances as too many for one letter. Three, with consequences, can be a BIFF. Six "do nots" cannot.

## Anti-patterns
- **Proving fault on a late delivery**: even if you have proof, the BIFF is the ship date and the arrival window.
- **Apologizing to a blamer as a reflex**: focus on the fix. An apology becomes ammunition.
- **Assuming the public will "see it was a prank"**: alarm shuts down that analysis. Answer quickly with credible facts.

## Worked Example
A mediation client cancels, says you did it wrong, and wants the fee back. His letter mentions his wife and his lawyer as agreeing. Nobody is copied.

BIFF, by letter, after a review: thank them for the concerns. You have done a large number of cases and you know styles differ. You are glad they found a fit. Best wishes. You do not recount their lateness or their phone calls. You do not argue the refund in this letter, because raising it and refusing it keeps the demand alive. You do not copy the lawyer. If a second letter demands the money, a later BIFF can refuse it in one firm sentence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Contain the client. Do not try to ban an entire personality type from your work.
2. If they did not show you a cc line, do not create one.
3. Future behavior plus a real consequence beats a list of sins.
4. Public blame needs a short factual answer before the feeling becomes the verdict.
