# BIFF: Quick Responses to High-Conflict People
**Author**: Bill Eddy | **Words**: ~39,000 | **Chapters**: 11 | **Edition**: 2nd (2014) | **Generated**: 2026-10-27

## How to Use This Skill

When the user pastes a hostile message and wants a reply, do this before writing:

1. Decide whether a reply is needed at all. Silence is often the better move.
2. Name the goal: manage the relationship, reduce it, or phase it out.
3. Draft one paragraph: Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm.
4. Strip admonishments, unsolicited advice, and substantive apologies.
5. Match the audience and the channel of the original attack.
6. If others were copied or the attack is public, answer those people with facts, fast.
7. Offer the draft for a human to send. Do not send it yourself.

- **Without arguments** — use the decision rules below.
- **With a topic** — read the chapter that owns it before answering.
- **With a chapter** — load `ch05` or whichever chapter was named.
- **Coaching a draft** — use the 10 questions in [ch10](chapters/ch10-coaching.md). Ask them in order. Save your own edits for the last question, and only if asked.

When a topic is not in Core Frameworks, read the chapter file before answering.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

Use these while writing. Exact names matter.

### BIFF

**Brief. Informative. Friendly. Firm.**

- **Brief**: one paragraph, usually 2–5 sentences. Length of their attack does not set your length. Extra sentences are extra hooks.
- **Informative**: one or two sentences of neutral, useful facts. Shift the topic from opinions about each other to something that can be decided. No defense of your character.
- **Friendly**: open with thanks, acknowledgment, or a plain "Okay, [name]." Close on a neutral note ("Have a nice weekend") when the relationship continues. Sarcasm counts as unfriendly.
- **Firm**: end the thread, or give two clear choices and a reply date. "If I don't have X by [date], I will do Y. I hope that won't be necessary." That sentence is firm and friendly together.

Have someone else read the draft. Reviewers usually cut it in half. What you leave out matters more than what you add.

Say to yourself, before typing: "This is not about me." "The issue's not the issue." "The personality is the issue." You do not have to prove who you are.

### When to stay silent

Do not reply when all of these are true enough:

- Only the two of you are in the thread.
- There is no real issue (their personality is the issue).
- It is only an opinion about your character, memory, ethics, or looks.
- You will not change their view.
- You already answered this subject.

### When to answer, and answer fast

Answer when other people can treat silence as agreement. High-conflict people (HCPs) often assume you agree unless you disagree quickly. They retell the story. Intense emotion makes the story feel true to listeners.

Reply to the same people, in the same format: email to email, Reply All if it was a group, a public note if it was public. If you cannot safely use their channel (a radio show that already laughed at you), use a channel you control and reach the same community.

Do not widen the audience. If they claim "my lawyer agrees" but did not copy anyone, do not add those people. Make it small. Many HCPs invent allies.

### Three relationship goals

Pick one before you write. The words change with the goal.

- **Manage** — you work with them, co-parent, or cannot leave. Keep contact, keep it dull, focus on the next behavior.
- **Reduce** — keep what is useful, limit the intensity. Say no to a specific request. Do not write the essay about the relationship.
- **End** — phase out. Abrupt rejection often increases contact (harassment, stalking, lawsuits). Wind down by refusing specific requests. Skip the long "why we are done" talk. A counselor can structure a real ending if you need one.

### Private working theory

You do not need to decide whether someone is an HCP. If you suspect it, write more carefully. HCP is a description, not a diagnosis. Do not tell them they are an HCP or that they have a personality disorder. Use the theory to change your behavior, not theirs.

HCP pattern, when it repeats: all-or-nothing thinking, unmanaged emotions, extreme behavior, preoccupation with blaming a target. They often lack awareness of their part and do not try to change it. Pointing that out escalates. "Look in the mirror" is counter-Blamespeak. It makes you look like the problem to outsiders.

### The 3 A's — leave them out

- **Admonishments**: "You should know better." "I'm surprised you'd even consider that." Judging the person, even gently, is read as an attack.
- **Advice**: suggestions they did not ask for. "Constructive feedback" they didn't request still puts you above them.
- **Apologies**: "I'm sorry I hurt you by doing X" is heard as "it is all my fault," then reused later. Social niceties are fine: "I'm sorry I'm late." "I'm sorry you're in this situation."

### Negative advocates

HCPs recruit other people to carry their anger. Those people are usually emotionally hooked and uninformed. Inform them quickly, in the same channel, with a short factual note. Do not attack the HCP in that note. Invite questions to you. Most bystanders then stay out. Anger at the bystanders is wasted; they did not have the facts.

### Indirect confrontation

Use this when you need different future behavior and a direct scolding will fail.

1. State the desired future behavior. Skip the character verdict.
2. Tie it to a policy, schedule, or shared constraint, not to "you are irresponsible."
3. If the past must be mentioned, say it once, then spend the rest on what happens next.

**Negative feedback** fails with HCPs when it judges the whole person, uses a harsh tone, or stays in the past. You are not rewarding bad behavior by skipping the lecture. You are teaching a behavior they can actually do.

### Limits without threats

Threats ("If you don't stop, I will…") feel good for a few minutes and trigger defensiveness. Informing about consequences is the same limit in a calm voice: "The code requires a Notice of Violation if this isn't cleaned up in a reasonable time. If you give me a date, we can avoid the notice."

**Two choices**: "The easy way is X. The hard way is Y. You will get through this faster the easy way." Give a short decision window. If they cannot choose, that is a choice; use the hard way.

**Do not name the threat in the reply.** "Don't come back or you'll be arrested" makes them picture coming back. Handle safety off the page (security, police, a hotline). In the BIFF, remove the reason they would show up (send the document they demanded) and do not mention the scene.

### Contain, don't chase

After a firing or a blow-up, a short calm reply can hold the contact in email instead of at the door or in public. Ignore the emotional hooks. Answer the practical request. Do not try to win the feelings.

### Stay on your question

HCPs often ignore your question and open a new attack. Connect to the real concern in one clause, then return to your request. If they keep poking: "I've said all I'm going to say on this subject." Then stop.

### Suicide talk

Take it seriously every time. A prior plan from a counselor, a hotline, or the police is the response, not a debate about the original request. Do not let the statement steer you into doing what they want on the unrelated issue. If you don't know what to do, contact a hotline or a counselor before you improvise.

### Coaching

There is no single correct BIFF. It depends on who writes it, who receives it, and the situation. The 10 questions are in [ch10](chapters/ch10-coaching.md). The writer decides. The coach's own ideas come last, only if invited, and as two or three options, not a rewrite.

## Scope & Limits

This skill is synthesized practice guidance from the book. It is not the book text, not legal advice, not a mental-health diagnosis, and not a reason to label someone. HCP is Eddy's descriptive term. Only a qualified clinician diagnoses a personality disorder, and this skill never tells you to deliver that label.

If someone may harm themselves or someone else, contact local emergency services or a suicide hotline. Do not treat a BIFF as the safety plan.

For a hands-on draft, combine this skill with the actual message. For topics outside this book, say so.
